# Video Upscale Models on Higgsfield — Which One to Use

Research date: 2026-09-19. Sources listed at the end.

Scope: the three engines in the Higgsfield **Video Upscale** selector — ByteDance
Upscale, FLUX.3 Upscale, and Topaz Video — judged against what this drive
actually produces: 1080x1920 vertical clips, 24 fps, 4–5 seconds, generated by
seedance 2.5 from a still starting frame, cut in CapCut and posted organically
to TikTok and Instagram.

---

## The short answer

**ByteDance Upscale, with the `aigc` preset, is the right engine for this
pipeline.** It is the cheapest of the three by a factor of four to sixteen, it
is the only one of the three that is non-generative (it enhances what is in the
frame instead of re-inventing it), and it is the only one that also handles
frame-rate interpolation in the same pass. Everything already sitting in
`clips/upscaled/` on this drive was produced by it — 1440x2560 at 30 fps — and
that is the correct call, not an accident worth revisiting.

Topaz Video is the escalation, not the default. FLUX.3 is the best-reviewed of
the three in the abstract and the wrong economics for organic volume.

---

## What each engine actually is

### ByteDance Upscale

Higgsfield's ByteDance engine runs on BytePlus VOD, the same enhancement stack
ByteDance uses on its own platforms. It is preset-driven rather than
prompt-driven: you pick a content type (`common`, `aigc`, `short_series`, `ugc`,
`old_film`), a target resolution up to 8K, and a frame rate of 24, 30 or 60.
The `aigc` preset exists specifically for AI-generated source material, which is
exactly what this drive feeds it.

The important property is that it is **not a diffusion model**. It sharpens,
denoises, deblocks and enlarges what the source already contains. It does not
hallucinate texture. For product work that matters more than it sounds — a
diffusion upscaler is free to rewrite a box label, re-letter the printed lid, or
decide the projector's mesh lens disc should have a different weave. ByteDance
will not do that, because inventing detail is not in its job description.

It also offers three internal tiers: fast, standard, and pro. Pro uses
large-model restoration, is the only tier returning 10-bit or 12-bit output, and
costs roughly ten times standard. For 9:16 phone-screen delivery, standard is
the tier that makes sense.

Independent testing on fal put its rate at $0.0072 per second for 1080p output
at 30 fps — the lowest per-second rate of any of the ten video upscalers
benchmarked there, by a wide margin.

The related open-weights ByteDance model, SeedVR2, was independently ranked #1
for overall restoration quality and temporal consistency among free upscalers in
2026, and reviewers testing it against Topaz noted it "felt more comfortable" on
AI-generated footage because it is calibrated for the artifacts generative video
actually produces. That is the same research lineage the hosted ByteDance
upscaler comes from, which is a reason to trust it on seedance output
specifically.

### FLUX.3 Upscale

Black Forest Labs shipped FLUX Video Upscale as a standalone tool on
2026-08-19/20, and Higgsfield added it shortly after. It enlarges a source
1.5x, 2x or 3x — from an HD input that lands at roughly 1080p, 2K and 4K — and
preserves the original audio track.

It has two modes, and the distinction is the whole product:

- **Precise** (creativity 0, 4 diffusion steps): preserves the source exactly
  and sharpens it. BFL explicitly recommends it "when you need to keep identity
  or reference details consistent" — faces, products, brand assets.
- **Creative** (creativity 1, 8 steps, and the *default*): pushes repair and
  detail generation. BFL's own note is that it "can change or replace identity,
  so reference consistency is lower than with Precise."

That default is a trap for product work. A request that carries only a video
returns a 2x creative upscale.

FLUX.3's genuine advantage is that it understands FLUX 3 output natively and
fixes failure modes a general-purpose upscaler misses — smudged faces, gridded
artifacts on water and grass. In fal's ten-way head-to-head it placed second
overall, behind only Topaz Astra 2. Its input limits are the tightest of the
three: MP4 only, 20 seconds maximum, 50 MB maximum.

**Practical blocker for this drive:** the Higgsfield MCP `upscale_video` tool
exposes only `bytedance` and `topaz` as providers. FLUX.3 upscaling has to be
driven by hand in the web UI. For a 376-clip pipeline that is not a small
detail.

### Topaz Video

Higgsfield's Topaz engine is the diffusion side of the Topaz catalogue —
Higgsfield's own documentation describes it as "diffusion-based enhancement that
can add new detail, not just recover what's already there," with Starlight
Precise named as an example preset, plus scale factor, an enhancement toggle,
auto/manual parameters, and frame interpolation.

Topaz's lineup splits cleanly by intent, and knowing which half you are in is
the whole game:

- **Starlight Precise 2.5/2.6** is the *corrective* model, built to reduce the
  plastic, artificial look of GenAI video. It strengthens exactly what
  generators get subtly wrong — faces, fabric, material response, small text and
  logos — while staying faithful to what is there.
- **Astra 2** is the *creative* model. It generates new detail and texture, has
  a creativity slider and an optional prompt, performs best on stylized content
  and detail-sparse scenes, and is — per Topaz's own documentation — explicitly
  **not** for photoreal fidelity work. Topaz's guidance: if the output must stay
  faithful, such as a client's face or a product label, creative diffusion is the
  wrong tool, because it invents.

Astra 2 shipped April 2026 and is a real step forward on the over-processed
plastic look of earlier Starlight variants. It took the #1 slot in fal's ten-way
test. But reviewers also found Astra "adds stylized texture to live action that
looks wrong," and Topaz's diffusion models are slow — roughly real-time or
slower, with fal's tester logging 1+ minute waits per clip.

The Higgsfield Topaz engine is the one to reach for when a source is genuinely
soft or a face has gone plastic and the fix requires rebuilding detail rather
than sharpening it. It is not the one to reach for on an approved packshot.

## Cost, which is the deciding factor here

Higgsfield's own published pricing for a 10-second 4K upscale:

| Engine    | Credits (10s, 4K) | Approx. cost | Relative to ByteDance |
| --------- | ----------------- | ------------ | --------------------- |
| ByteDance | 8                 | $0.40        | 1x                    |
| Topaz     | 35                | $1.75        | 4.4x                  |
| FLUX.3    | 132               | $6.60        | 16.5x                 |

Scaled to this drive's actual unit — a 5-second clip — and to the 376 clips
currently sitting in the LED curtain product folder:

| Engine    | Per 5s clip (4K) | 376 clips |
| --------- | ---------------- | --------- |
| ByteDance | ~$0.20           | ~$75      |
| Topaz     | ~$0.88           | ~$329     |
| FLUX.3    | ~$3.30           | ~$1,241   |

At 2K — which is what the existing upscaled clips on this drive are, and which is
already past what a phone screen resolves — the ByteDance figure roughly halves
again, to around $0.10 per clip.

The gap is not a rounding difference. Choosing FLUX.3 as the pipeline default
would cost more than the entire rest of the content operation.

## The argument that upscaling barely matters here at all

Worth stating plainly because it changes how much any of this is worth arguing
about. The sources are already 1080x1920 — native 1080p vertical, which is the
resolution TikTok and Instagram serve. Upscaling to 2K or 4K buys headroom for
CapCut crops, zooms and stabilisation, and it buys a slightly cleaner texture
after the platform's own re-encode. It does not buy a better ad.

Independent 2026 benchmarking on short-form is blunt about this: for TikTok,
Reels and Shorts viewed on a phone, hook strength, caption readability and
posting consistency move the numbers, and production polish does not. Several
reviewers concluded that for phone-screen delivery, a heavy upscaler is not
worth the render time.

So the correct posture is: cheap, fast, non-destructive, run on everything —
which is ByteDance — and reserve the expensive engines for the rare clip where
something is visibly broken.

## Recommendation for this drive

**Default, run on everything:** ByteDance Upscale, `aigc` preset, standard tier,
2K target, 30 fps. This is what the existing `clips/upscaled/` files already
are. Keep it. Go to 4K only for a clip that will be heavily cropped or zoomed in
CapCut.

Via the MCP, that is `provider: "bytedance"`, `preset: "aigc"`,
`resolution: "2k"`, `fps: 30`, with `width: 1080` and `height: 1920` passed
explicitly (the ByteDance path requires the source dimensions).

**Escalation, one clip at a time:** Topaz Video, when a face has gone plastic,
when grain or flicker survived the generation, or when a hero clip is soft
enough that sharpening alone will not save it. Expect a slower render and a
roughly 4.4x bill. Do **not** run it on a clip whose value is an approved
packshot — the LED curtain's black wire, the windowless box, the projector's
mesh lens disc are all things a diffusion model is licensed to reinterpret.

**Not worth adopting:** FLUX.3, for this pipeline. It is genuinely good —
second overall in the best head-to-head test available — but at 16.5x the cost,
with a 20-second/50 MB input ceiling, a creative-by-default mode that rewrites
product detail unless explicitly set to Precise, and no MCP coverage so every
job is a manual web-UI pass. The quality delta over ByteDance is not visible on
a phone at 1080p.

**One flag worth knowing about:** if a source clip ever needs *slow motion* or a
frame-rate change as well as an upscale, Topaz's Apollo interpolation runs in
the same job. ByteDance's fps parameter does interpolate too, but note that
setting it above 30 doubles the cost.
